# Growing a Digital Garden

For years, I maintained a traditional blog. New posts appeared in reverse chronological order. Each piece was meant to be polished, complete, a finished product. The pressure was paralyzing. I wrote less and less.

Then I discovered the concept of digital gardens, and everything changed.

## What Is a Digital Garden?

Unlike a blog, a digital garden isn't organized by time. Ideas are planted as seeds—rough notes, half-formed thoughts—and cultivated over time. Some grow into full essays. Others remain seedlings indefinitely.

There's no pressure to finish. No expectation of polish. Just ideas, growing in public.

## The Key Principles

### 1. Work in Public

Traditional blogging hides the process. You only see the final product. A digital garden shows the evolution of thinking. Early drafts, revisions, contradictions—all visible.

### 2. Topography Over Timeline

Posts aren't sorted by date. They're connected by topic, by association, by whatever links make sense. Navigation is exploratory rather than sequential.

### 3. Imperfection Is Expected

Blog posts should be complete. Garden notes can be stubs—placeholders for ideas you'll develop later. Readers understand they're seeing work in progress.

## How I've Changed

Since adopting this approach:

- **I write more** - Lower stakes mean fewer barriers
- **I think better** - Writing clarifies half-formed ideas
- **I connect more** - Linking ideas reveals patterns
- **I share more** - No need to wait for perfection

## Tools Don't Matter (Much)

You can build a digital garden with any tool: Notion, Obsidian, a static site generator, even plain HTML files. The philosophy matters more than the implementation.

That said, bidirectional links help. Being able to see what links to a note (not just what it links to) encourages organic connection.

## Getting Started

1. Pick one idea you've been thinking about
2. Write 100 words about it, rough and unedited
3. Publish it somewhere, marked as a draft or seedling
4. Link to it when you write related pieces
5. Return to tend it when inspiration strikes

Stop waiting for ideas to be finished. Plant them now and watch them grow.
